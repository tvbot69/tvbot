marsb Feb 21 2019
Expanding the few hooks of Come On Pilgrim ("Caribou," "Elevate Me"), the band write some of the greatest hooks of their careers:

  • ”I GOT A BROKEN FAIIIIII—I GOTTA—I GOT A BROKEN FACE UH HUH UH HUH UH HUH OOOH!”

  • ”OOOOOOH—“ “Stop!” “AH—“

  • ”THIS IS A SONG ABOUT A SUPERHERO NAMED TONY. IT’S CALLED TONY’S THEME!”

  • *Music stops.* ”YOUR BONE’S GOTTA LITTLE MACHINE!” *Riff comes back.* “AHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHH!!!!”
(Yeah, I'm counting the two-second intro to "Where Is My Mind???" as a hook because it's been forever ingrained in my head since the first time I heard it.)

A lot of artists sound like the Pixies, which is expected given Nirvana taking Doolittle's quiet-loud dynamics and popularizing it on Nevermind, but those artists sound like the Pixies of Doolittle-onwards; no one sounds like Surfer Rosa. This is true even when Nirvana teamed up with Steve Albini to recreate the rawness of Surfer Rosa that he loved so much, and even when Frank Black returned to the short song format on sophomore solo album Teenager of the Year. (As far as Frank Black's solo albums go, I'd sooner lean to his debut which at least has a few good songs than Teenager, which has "Headache" and very little else. Surfer Rosa has a strangeness permeated throughout that's hard to imitate, achieved because many of the songs are so short - most of 'em aren't even songs - that they often feel like passing weirdness, in part because of Steve Albini's touch (with a lot of attention on the drums, leaving the other elements feel dream-like, but a dark dream) and in part because of Frank Black's lyrics (ie. "Where Is My Mind???" evokes talking fish that we ought to stay away from because the fish swears).

In that regard, this is the album that put Steve Albini on the map as an in-demand studio presence, to the point that even fey folksy types like Joanna Newsom and bluegrass artists like Robbie Fulks would tap him for their albums. On follow-up and Albini-less Doolittle (who left the band after calling them a bunch of cows who needed guidance...which he regretted later), then-producer Gil Norton will focus more on the vocals. On songs like "Broken Face" or "Tony's Theme," they're definitely the prominent element, but on something like “Bone Machine," Frank Black's screaming is barely noticeable unless you're looking for it especially since they come during a time when there’s descending guitar riffs and bass-lines to worry about, not to mention Lovering moving from keeping time to explosive drum rolls.

Like Doolittle, there's a drop in quality here, except it's exaggerated in the album's final stretch: ”You Fuckin' Die...!" (separated into its own track from "Oh My Golly!!!" on reissues) and “I’m Amazed” feature some studio banter between the members of the band (Albini’s touch) which turn out to be the only interesting moments of the album after “Tony's Theme.” (I guess those harmonies on "Oh My Golly!!!" are catchy?) ”Vamos” is a re-write of the one found on Come On Pilgrim with more electric guitar, but it wasn’t very interesting the first time and it’s not very interesting now, and it doesn't help that it's 4 minutes long on an album comprised primarily of 2-minute songs. The only song after "Tony's Theme" that I return to semi-regularly is closer "Brick is Red," and that's mostly because of the guitar-work.

The guitar-work is also the draw of some of the early songs too, like the surf guitar of "Something Against You"; "Break My Body" is especially stitched together, and a half-memorable hook outside of the tasty guitar lines. So you see, very flawed album, and that applies to the entirety of the Pixies catalog, including the aforementioned Come On Pilgrim EP which is assuredly not the best indie rock EP of the 1980s (which would be R.E.M.'s Chronic Town). Not pulling punches here, their legacy has a lot to do with the fact that they put out a very succinct (and thus, consistent) discography, and had they stuck together, their discography would look similar to U2 or R.E.M. as evidence by Frank Black's solo output and the Breeders, even before taking their laughable reunion output into account. On that note, I can't imagine forking over whatever they're charging to see them now in a big arena or open space, considering that one of my favorite moments here - David Lovering's opening drum-roll after the intro of "Where Is My Mind???" that announces that razor-sharp guitar line - is meant to be heard as close as possible; more of a headphones album than any of their other stuff.

I mentioned that these 13/14 tracks barely constitute as songs, with the exceptions of "Gigantic" and "Where Is My Mind???", which is part of the middle stretch of the album from "Broken Face" through "Tony's Theme" where the album really shines. On that note, I really dig Pitchfork's Mike Powell's description of the album in their review of the whole catalog in lieu of reviewing Indie Cindy (though the score(s) they gave are utterly ridiculous): "Many of its songs feel half-finished or regurgitated in half-digested form, with verses that run on longer than they ordinarily might, choruses that repeat odd numbers of times instead of even ones, and abrupt shifts in tone and volume." Don't discount the 'half-finished' songs: not enough is said about "River Euphrates," where the band explores the contrast between Frank Black's unhinged vocals and Kim Deal's sweeter ones that they'll really lean on for follow-up Doolittle (think "Tame"), or "Cactus," which is one of the few times this band made a ballad and whose quietness feels unique to the point that it sounds like a mistake on an album as often bracing as this one; you really have to turn it up to hear Black's sexual yearning. (David Bowie would cover "Cactus" on 'comeback' album Heathen, a standout on that album because it's the one with a unique melody/set of lyrics.) And some of my favorite tracks have always been the rush of "Broken Face" and "Tony's Theme" (the rare sort of song that makes you wish you were born with a different first name).

The best song, of course, is "Where Is My Mind???"; if you have ever made plans to "jam" with a friend because both of you own acoustic guitars, but your plans always fall through because neither of you actually know how to play the guitar very well, fear no more. “Where Is My Mind???” is a testament to what you can do with two guitars, five chords (technically six, but I won't count the Am that sneaks in there), and a simple riff. Sure, you guys probably won’t be able to replicate Kim Deal’s wordless backing vocals that give the track a psychedelic edge (once the riff stops, it sounds like it’s coming from underwater), but you’ll at least be able to shout “YEAH” in unison. The script of the ending of Fight Club probably read something like this: “A building crumbles. Marla lets out a gasp, her hand slips into Mr. Narrator’s. Buildings topple around them. “Where Is My Mind???” starts playing. Nothing else matters.

A-

Kate_McColl Oct 25 2011
After releasing a stellar debut EP in Come On Pilgrim the previous year, on March 21, 1988 the Pixies released their first full length LP. This is a fantastic album, and proved to be one of the monumental alternative rock albums of the 1980s, let alone of all time. It's no wonder why this album has been heralded by later rock legends like Nirvana and Radiohead. It was after the release of this record that the Pixies became a force to be reckoned with, finding success in America's indie circuit, and also having quite a bit of success in Britain.

Steve Albini was brought in to produce, and he does an excellent job. One thing I always love about Albini's production is the way the drums sound, they always sound so heavy and so clear. Steve has always said he likes to capture what bands sound like live, and I think he succeeded here. Listening to Surfer Rosa feels like you're sitting in on a Pixies rehearsal in a small practice space. With the little bit of studio banter in between songs and the raw production it gives a great intimate experience. Albini's mix is great, as there's not a lot of layers of instrumentation, you can hear every Pixie playing his or her part.

Now onto the Pixies themselves. There's such a cohesive nature to their playing on this record, as if the band are playing together, as opposed to recording separate tracks (this may be how it was recorded). They sound tight, but loose enough to just let go and have fun with these songs. Charles (Frank Black) Thompson's lyrics and vocal delivery is fantastic. It's very unique with lots of clever hooks, often going between talking, singing, singing in spanish, screaming, or a high pitched wail. Kim Deal's bass parts are simple but very catchy, and her backing vocals are mixed perfectly with Charles' creating the Pixies vocal sound. Joey Santiago's guitar playing is also great, nothing too fancy, but that's quite alright. His noise influence shines through with his well crafted leads, using his guitar as a color instrument, rather than relying on riffs. Keeping the pace going is drummer David Lovering, once again keeping it simple, but comes up with great beats to get his band going.

As for the songs, I could probably go through every track, but I'll just throw a few in here. Upon playing the album, immediately we're treated to the great drum sound of Steve Albini played by Lovering, "Bone Machine" is the first track. Featuring a great riff by Santiago and more of Charles' exceptional lyrics. "Gigantic" is one of my favorites, sung by the lovely Kim Deal. One of her few vocal leads in the Pixies discography, "Gigantic" tells the tale of a voyeur witnessing an interracial couple having sex. It's got a great bass riff, and Kim's voice is great (check out The Breeders for more of Kim). "Where is My Mind?" is the most talked about song on the record, and probably the most talked about Pixies track. Featuring a hypnotizing lead by Santiago backed by ambient vocals by Deal (which were recorded in a bathroom for a natural echo effect), the song has every right to be their most popular track. Charles' vocals and lyrics are superb, painting a surrealistic image of swimming in the Caribbean. Once again it's got the great drum sound pounding through.

Another thing that makes the Pixies so fantastic is their minimalist approach to songwriting. Much like early Wire, the band doesn't get indulgent with their songs. Eight of the thirteen tracks here are under three minutes, and the longest track is four minutes and eighteen seconds! I saw an interview with Kim Deal talking about how the Pixies would just end songs. No build ups to a finishing drum solo, arena rock end. They'd just end. Their songs hardly feature a guitar solo of any sort, if so they last a few seconds, unless you count "Vamos" with it's noise guitar break, but that's it. Their songs get to the point, and if they need to end, they will. See "Break My Body" for example. The last verse of the song sounds like a bridge, well is it? I'm not sure as after the vocals are done the song is as well, it could have gone back to another chorus, but why? Does it need to? No it doesn't. This is the genius of the Pixies, knowing what's needed and what's not, and making just great songs.

I can't give enough credit to the production and the overall feel this album gives off. It's so fun, but not in anyway immature or dumb, it's just fantastic. The band sounds like they're having the time of their lives, and Steve Albini captured this perfectly. The Pixies would go on to record yet another masterpiece the following year with Doolittle, thus further cementing them to their now god-like status. This is an album that you hear and immediately want to go pick up a guitar and start jamming, it's inspiring, and I can see why people like Kurt Cobain held it to such high regard. One of the finest albums ever created, by one of the finest bands ever formed.

Favorite Tracks - Bone Machine, Break My Body, Gigantic, Where is My Mind?, Cactus, Vamos

*Got my copy signed by Frank, Joey, and David on November 9, 2011!!

Silent_Mike Dec 19 2006
College rock! Weird feeling over transmitted meaning. Partly due to my peers elevating these guys to Stones-like status, I've always harbored a hatred for the popularity wake the Pixies left. But that ain't their fault; they may have cultivated the... er, cult, but someone has to plunk down the dollar.

But yeah, this is a catchy record. For a debut, it's fairly remarkable. Most of the poses struck achieve some sort of resonance (the false hesitation on "Broken Face," the bi-curiosity narrative of "I'm Amazed," the bilingual mythmaking of "Oh My Golly!"), and the playing combines the repetition of hardcore punk with the hooks of... well, I suppose the Pixies pioneered this sort of rock 'n' roll. Play-acting at weirdness is reduced to a venial sin when the songs are of a quality, and except for large chunks at the end, this is a quality LP.

jaysonle Nov 05 2006
I'll say what every other human being has already stated about this album - it's brilliant! The "soft-loud" formula that Kurt Cobain so generously gave props to numerous times throughout his life goes directly to the Pixies. There are many arguments that the Pixies didn't create this style of playing, but who cares this album is a rip-roaring good time. From the opening of "Bone Machine" to the sweet and funny "Gigantic" these songs are a blast. Every last song is unblemished and I want to find one single person who can honestly say that don't enjoy "Where Is My Mind?." It's just an aneurysm of sound that screeches at times, but still carries out with a lovely melody. You can't go wrong with this album. Highly recommended!!
